Frequently Asked Questions on Valuation 
1 General 

1.1 Why is there a need for the market to correct its mistakes? 
By investing money in anything, we are foregoing purchasing power today to get higher 
purchasing power in the future. So, the only reason to invest money today is to get money 
back which would allow us to buy more goods in the future. There are only two ways in which 
this can be achieved from an investment: 
• Capital return (through dividends and buy-backs); and 
• Capital appreciation. 
Suppose the market is undervaluing a firm which is generating immense amounts of cash 
flow. If the market does not realise its mistake, one way for an investor to get his money back 
would be for the firm to declare a huge special dividend.  But, as a passive investor, the 
investor may not be able to affect the dividend policy of the firm. Therefore, he is highly reliant 
on the capital markets to correct their mistakes. 
Also, in today’s world, only a fraction of the total return from stocks comprises dividends. Most 
return comes from capital appreciation, which obviously is dependent on the market correcting 
its mistakes. 
And then, you also think hard about opportunity costs.  To sit on a stock for 10 years without 
it doing much is easier said than done, let alone for 20-30 years.  And in any case, if the 
markets are not doing what they are supposed to do as per the model for such a long time, it 
is highly probable that the mistake is with the model and not the market. 

1.2 How do you decide whether to use certainty equivalent cash flows which can be 
discounted at the risk-free rate vs. risk adjusted discount rate? 
The debate between using certainty equivalent cash flows vs. risk adjusted discount rate 
arises from the fact that Warren Buffett uses certainty equivalent cash flows and discounts 
them at the risk-free rate of the economy. The reason cited for the above is that it eliminates 
the need to estimate a discount rate. 
However, this approach is becoming harder to follow today because of the following reasons:  
• Gradual shift in the composition of the cash flows of companies. If you look at the concept 

of a financial balance sheet, a larger proportion of cash flows are now reliant on growth 
assets than existing assets.  

• Secondly, increase in macro-economic risks since the global financial crisis has also made 
it more difficult to assess certainty equivalent cash flows for a company.

2 DCF: Risk free rates 

2.1 What is a risk-free return? 
A risk-free return is one where the actual return is equal to the expected return, i.e. there is no 
variance around expected return. 
For an investment to be risk free, it has to satisfy the following two conditions: 
• It should not have any default risk; and 
• It should not carry any reinvestment risk. 
The following three elements form the foundation for thinking about risk-free rates: 
• Time horizon: Risk free rates depend on the timing of the cash flows. For instance, for a 

3-month cash flow, the correct risk-free rate would be the 91-day T-Bill. 
• Currency: Risk free rates are currency specific. 
• Not all governments are risk-free: Some governments carry default risk and thus, the 

local currency bond rate cannot be considered as the risk-free rate for the country. 

2.2 Why do we use a 10-year T-Bond rate to calculate the risk-free rate? 
One of the conditions of a risk-free rate is that the investment should not carry any 
reinvestment risk.  Thus, we need to match the duration of the riskless asset to the duration 
of the cash flows that we discount. For instance, if we are assessing a cash flow 3 months 
from now, the correct risk-free rate would be the yield on a 91-day T-Bill.  
When we value the equity in a company on a going concern basis, we are estimating cash 
flows for long periods of time (sometimes into infinity) and consequently, we should use the 
longest government bond rates available. In many instances, this would be the 30-year 
government bond rate. 
However, we generally end up restricting ourselves to the 10-year government bond rate to 
compute the risk-free rate. The reason we do this is because using the 10-year rate makes 
our life a lot easier as we move along in the valuation process. If the end game was to only 
come up with the cost of equity, we would not hesitate to use the 30-year rate as the risk-free 
rate. But, a cost of equity is only one of the components of the valuation process. As we move 
along, we also need to compute a cost of debt. And the cost of debt is derived as follows:

compare the concerned country’s PRS score with other countries which have a similar PRS 
and assign it a default spread based on the average of default spreads of those countries.

2.7 The concept of real risk-free rates and some other issues? 
Real risk-free rates should be used only when discounting real cash flows. Generally, a real 
risk-free rates and real cash flows are used when the inflation is abnormally high in the 
currency in which you wish to do a valuation. TIPS rate is an example of a real risk-free rate. 
A security will provide a real risk-free return if:  
• it does not carry default risk; and  
• guarantees a real return. 
Can the TIPS rate be used as the real-risk free rate across countries? 
The TIPS rate can be used as a real-risk free rate across countries as long as we assume that 
capital flows freely. This is because a country offering a higher ‘real’ return will attract capital 
pushing those rates down towards equilibrium. For instance, let’s assume that the real return 
in US is 2% while it is 5% in India. If you are an investor in US assets, you will likely take 
money out of the US and start investing in India instead. When many investors start doing this, 
yields on Indian assets will start to go down and those on US assets will start rising, with the 
consequence that an equilibrium will be reached with no country offering an "extra" real return. 
Hence, it is possible to use the TIPS rate as the real risk-free rate across countries. 
However, the assumption about free capital flows may not be appropriate in case of some 
countries. An alternative approach for such a country would be to set the real riskless rate as 
the ‘Expected real growth rate in long term’.

2.9 Should you normalise risk-free rates? 
No, risk free rates should never be normalized.  The current risk-free rate acts as an actual 
opportunity cost of investing capital in riskier securities, TODAY. Thus, if you find equities as 
a class to be overvalued based on the choice of a risk-free rate then, you forgo investing in 
equities and invest your funds AT THE RISK-FREE RATE. However, if you cannot invest at a 
‘normalised’ risk-free rate.  
For example, suppose the current risk-free rate is 2% but the average risk-free rate over the 
previous 5 years is 4%. If an investor uses 4% as their risk-free rate, they might find equities 
to be overvalued (a higher discount rate leads to lower value, other things remaining constant). 
If they then decide to not invest in equities, they will not be able to invest risk-free at 4% 
because risk-free rates at that time are 2%.

3 DCF: Equity Risk Premiums 

3.1 What is an equity risk premium? 
An equity risk premium is the price of risk. It represents that rate which needs to be offered 
over and above the risk-free rate to lure investors to invest in equities as a class.  
An equity risk premium is a receptacle (bucket) of every hope and every fear regarding equities 
in the markets at a particular point in time. 
It is a function of: 
• How risk averse investors are collectively; and 
• How much risk they see in the average equity. 

3.2 What are the ways of measuring an equity risk premium? 
There are two ways in which an equity risk premium can be measured: 
• Historical risk premium; and  
• Implied equity risk premium. 
Historical risk premium: It is the premium that stocks have historically earned over riskless 
securities. It is sensitive to: 
• How far back in history should we go? 
• Whether to use T-Bills or T-Bonds? 
• Whether to use geometric or arithmetic averages? 
Implied equity risk premium: Implied equity risk premium looks at what people are actually 
paying for bearing the risk of investing in equities. Although it is volatile, it is a forward-looking 
number and dynamic. 

3.3 What are the potential problems with using a historical risk premium? 
A historical risk premium suffers from the following problems: 
• It is highly subjective in that the person computing must make choices. 
• It is backward looking. 
• High standard errors resulting in wide ranges for the premiums to be statistically 

significant. 
• Estimates move counter intuitively, i.e., down after a crisis and up after prosperity. 
• Suffers from survivorship bias when computed based only on US data (because US was 

the most successful market of the 20th century. Using it means you are assuming that the 
market for which you are computing the risk premium is going to be the best performing 
market going forward); and 

• Assumes mean reversion. This may or may not be true.

For Private Use Only 
3.4 What is an implied equity risk premium and how is it computed? 

An implied equity risk premium is a forward looking premium that is derived by looking at what 
market participants are actually demanding given the following inputs: 
• The level of the index; 
• The cash flows; 
• The growth in those cash flows; and 
• The risk-free rate of return. 

Computation of implied equity risk premiums: 
The calculation of implied equity risk premiums uses the framework of discounting cash flows 
on a coupon bond to come up with the yield of that bond where: 
• The current market price is set equal to the current level of the index (e.g. S&P 500). 
• The earnings on the index play the role of the coupons (only difference being that earnings 

can be volatile unlike coupons on a bond). These can be considered as any of the following: 
• The actual dividend + buyback yield of the index; 
• The earnings yield of the index; or 
• Yield calculated on the basis of free cash flow to equity of companies comprising the index. 
• Since the earnings are not guaranteed, we need to estimate a growth rate in these 

earnings. Note that in the calculation of implied ERP, we use consensus growth rates 
instead of fundamental growth rates. 

• Once we have the above inputs, we can calculate the rate of return which equates the 
present value of the cash flows to the current level of the index (with the help of the goal 
seek function in excel). 

• Lastly, we need to deduct the risk-free rate from this number to come up with the estimate 
of the implied equity risk premium.  

Note that the process described above is only sufficient for calculating the equity risk premium 
for a market which does not have any default risk (i.e., AAA-rated countries also called mature 
markets). For countries that carry default risk, we need to add a country risk premium 
(discussed later). 

3.5 Why do we use consensus growth rate (instead of fundamental growth rate) to 
compute implied ERP? 
Implied ERP is one input in valuation where we look to what the market "is actually" doing 
rather than what it "should" be doing. 
Another way to think about the Implied ERP is that it is a measure of the risk averseness or 
otherwise of the market participants at a given point in time as opposed to an investor’s 
own risk averseness. And we can find that out only if the inputs going into the Implied ERP 
calculation come from the market itself. 
Basically, what matters in an Implied ERP is what others think about the equity markets 
collectively and not what you as an individual think about it. That is why if you see, all other

3.10 Should an investor be compensated for country risk? In other words, can 
country risk be diversified away? 
The argument that there should be no compensation for country risk because it can be 
diversified was used by Mckinsey in its valuation text.  However, this argument has now been 
dropped.  
Whether investors can diversify country risk is a matter of evidence. This argument may have 
worked in the past as economies were not correlated with one another. Globalisation, 
however, has changed this over the last couple of decades. As companies go global, 
correlations among countries have also started to rise with the consequence that it is much 
more difficult for investors to “diversify” country risk. Prof. Damodaran has this to say about 
the issue – Mumbai is no more immune to what happens in Sao Paolo. 
Accordingly, a component of country risk needs to be incorporated in the ERP. 

3.11 How can equity risk premiums help in deciding asset allocation? 
One possible application of implied equity risk premiums is in deciding the asset allocation – 
i.e., whether the equity risk premiums at a particular point in time are high enough for investors 
to invest in equities as a class.  
One can use the following measures to check whether the equity risk premium is 
commensurate or not:  
• Compare the implied equity risk premium to the default spreads of investment grade bonds. 

Obviously, the bonds should have a lower spread than the equity risk premium but there 
are times when the two have converged (during the dot-com boom), giving an indication 
that one should be cautious. 

• Another way is to compare equity risk premiums across markets if you are a global investor. 
For instance, you could compute an equity risk premium for the Indian market (based on 
relevant data from the Indian market) and see how it compares to the premium that you get 
by adding Indian country risk to a mature market premium. A big divergence between the 
two may indicate that Indian investors are not adequately providing for the country risk.

4.7 Can betas be negative? 

Yes, if an investment is exposed to a macroeconomic risk such that it does well if that risk 
unfolds (while the rest of the market does badly. Betas can be negative, but it is rare to have 
a negative beta stock (e.g. gold mining companies).  
If you get a beta that is negative for a business, then it is highly unusual and it is likely to be 
some kind of hedge against some macroeconomic risk. 

4.8 Is it ok to have a negative beta stock in your portfolio? 
One of the consequences of having a negative beta is that the investment’s expected return 
falls below the risk-free rate. A question then arises as to why would anyone settle for anything 
less than the risk-free rate? The reason investors would buy such a stock is because it is like 
buying insurance (when inflation goes up an investor hopes that his/ her gold mining stock 
goes up and protects the rest of the portfolio).  
Having a negative beta stock in a portfolio is OK as long as an investor is diversified. If an 
investor is not diversified and has all of his investment in a negative beta investment (such as 
gold), then it is like buying fire insurance on a property when he doesn’t own any property. 
When an investor has nothing to insure, he will keep paying the premiums, but the payoff will 
never come. 

4.9 Can betas be compared globally? 
Yes, because betas are measures of relative risk. So, the average beta across all US 
companies is 1 and average beta across all African companies is also 1. You can’t compare 
PE ratios/ interest rates across countries, but you can compare betas. 

4.10 The concept of marginal investor and why only consider market risk? 
How can a marginal investor compel managers to do anything? The board of 
directors can but replacing a board is not that easy. This is all you need. The 
marginal investors will sell their shares in the company if managers are too 
conservative (and consider total risk in the company when evaluating projects). That 
will push the stock price down. To the extent that managers care about the stock 
price, they will mend their ways and start looking at market risk. If they don’t, you 
have to hope that activist investors or acquirers show up and try to change 
management.

5 DCF: Cost of debt 

5.1 What is the definition of debt? 
For an item to be classified as debt, it has to meet three criteria: 
• Give rise to a contractual commitment, that has to be met in good times or bad; 
• The commitment is usually tax deductible; and 
• Failure to honour that commitment can cost you control over the business. 
Using this criterion, all interest-bearing commitments, short term and long term, are clearly 
debt (including leases). The following items may also be considered as debt, if they meet 
other criteria: 
• Accounts payable and supplier credit: Only if you are willing to make the implicit interest 

expenses (i.e. the discounts lost by using credit) explicit;  
• Under-funded pension and health care obligations: Only if there is a legal requirement 

that you cover the underfunding with fixed payments in future years.

5.6 Which tax rate (effective or marginal) should be used to compute the after-tax

5.7 Which marginal tax rate should be used to compute after-tax cost of debt in case 
of a global company having operations in various countries? 
The marginal tax rate for a global company will depend on where it decides to borrow money 
and not where it is incorporated. Generally, a global company would borrow money where 
the tax rate is highest (since that will yield the highest tax benefit from the interest deduction). 
If, however, we can get information as to where the company borrows money in its annual 
report, we should use the marginal tax rate of the country in which the lending institution is 
located. 

5.8 Can we consider accounts payables as debt? 
You are correct, deferred tax liabilities are not considered just as accounts payables, etc. are 
not considered. And one reason we don't consider accounts payables as debt is because we 
can't explicitly see the interest rate associated with them (in the form of lost discounts). And it 
is messy to try and estimate an interest expense for them. 
However, if you could explicitly see what is the interest cost of these liabilities, you can 
consider them as debt as well. These do get considered in our valuation in the form of lower 
operating income since their cost is included in the costs of doing business. For instance, lets

treat that as debt. We would have to add back this expense and increase our operating income 
(the same way we don't reduce finance costs of debt when computing operating income). This 
increased operating income would result in a higher value of operating assets and then we 
would reduce the value of accounts payables come up with the value of equity.  
Regarding your point of pension liabilities and contingent liabilities, you should definitely figure

provide for those liabilities) and deduct from value of operating assets to come up with the

6 DCF: Cost of capital 

6.1 Why are market value weights used in computation of cost of capital? 
The reason we use market values is because we take transaction driven view of discounted 
cash flow valuation, i.e. I would have to pay the market values in order to acquire this company 
today.  
In essence, every discounted cash flow valuation is an acquisition valuation. 
Some of the wrong reasons that you may come across for using market value weights are: 
• Markets are usually right: If you assume this, and then go and value a company and 

come up with a different value for the company, the implicit assumption nullifies itself. 
• Market values are easy to obtain: But so are book values. In fact, market value of debt 

may be harder to obtain than the book value of debt. 
• Book values are meaningless: They may not reflect the true picture, but they are not 

meaningless. 

6.2 Which value of debt should we deduct to come up with the value of equity - the 
market value of the debt or the book value of the debt? 
Note that we use the market value of the debt to compute the debt ratio in the capital structure 
to compute the cost of capital. This is one part. The other part is, once you have discounted 
the cash flows to come up with the value of operating assets, which value of debt should we 
deduct to come up with the value of equity - the market value of the debt or the book value of 
the debt? 
Now to answer that question, we need to distinguish between why we are doing the valuation 
- is it for liquidation purposes or for buying the company as a going concern. 
• Liquidation Value 

In this scenario, you should the use the BOOK VALUE of debt to deduct to come up with 
the value of the equity. The reason is simple - When you liquidate the company, you are 
obligated to pay the "Book Value" of debt back to the lender and whatever remains would 
be distributed to the shareholders. The lender would not be satisfied with a lower payment, 
simply because you come up with a lower market value of debt. 
 

• Going Concern Value 
To understand why we should deduct the market value of debt from the value of operating 
assets to come up with the value of equity, it would help to understand why is it that we get 
a "lower/ higher" market value of debt compared to its book value. The reason for the 
divergence is the interest rate on THAT debt vs. the prevailing interest rates at which the 
company would be able to raise the debt if it were to raise it today. 
If the interest rate on the debt you already have is HIGHER than the market interest rate, 
the market value of the debt will be higher than book value. This is the scenario where you 
say that the seller could argue that he is taking a haircut on his equity. And he should, 
simply because the buyer is going to be saddled with high interest-bearing debt when the 
market interest rates are low. 

Page 26 of 115 
 



For Private Use Only 
In real life, you wouldn't get into these problems as any rational seller would simply 
refinance (if he can) the debt obligations before getting into negotiations and the problem 
would be taken care of. 
On the other hand, If the interest rate on the debt you already have is LOWER than the 
market interest rate, the market value of the debt will be lower than book value. This is the 
scenario where you say that the buyer could argue that he is paying a premium when he 
would be liable to pay back the book value of debt at maturity and not the market value. 
But think about it - the new buyer would also get to enjoy the lower interest expenses 
for the life of the debt than if he were to go and borrow from the market at present. As a 
seller of your business, that should be your argument since you were able to secure 
financing at a lower rate and you should be compensated for that. 
In practical life, the negotiation would depend on who has the upper hand! 
 

• Special case of DISTRESS 
There is one special case, in which a company's debt is trading at much lower than the 
book value of the debt because of DISTRESS. In this scenario, if as a buyer, you (a healthy 
company) are acquiring control of this company, you should use book value of debt 
since as soon as you acquire the company, its debt will start trading close to book 
value simply because now it has the backing of a healthy company and the default 
risk shrinks. The debt was trading below book not because of the differential between 
interest rates, but because of the heightened default risk of the company. 

6.3 Why use each year’s cost of capital to discount cash flows? 
The debate essentially is whether to discount the terminal value at the terminal value discount 
rate or to discount at each year’s different discount rate separately. Here’s my thoughts on the 
issue: 
The cost of capital is an opportunity cost of investing in an asset that yields cash flows in the 
future. Inverting this problem and thinking in future value terms would help a great deal. Let’s 
take a simple example with the following information – invest in a project which has a cash 
outflow of $100 today and a one-time cash inflow of $130 in year 5. Also suppose this asset 
is an extremely low risk asset which can be discounted at the risk-free rate. Our expectation 
of the risk-free rates over the next 5 years is as follows: 

Year 1 8% 
Year 2 7% 
Year 3 6% 
Year 4 5% 
Year 5 4% 

Now our opportunity cost is to invest the $100 in risk free rates each year instead of investing 
in this project. In that case, we get a value of $133.76 in year 5. Thus, comparing to the cash 
flow of $130 of the project, we should not be investing in it. 
However, if we use only the year 5 risk free rate to discount the cash flow of $130 in year 5, 
we will end up overestimating the present value of that cash flow.

7.2 Why are tax savings from interest ignored when computing FCFF6? 
The formula used for computation of FCFF ignores the tax savings from interest expenses. In 
effect, when computing FCFF, we pretend as if there is no debt in the firm and hence no 
interest expenses.  
The reason for ignoring the tax savings from interest expenses is because these savings are 
considered in the cost of capital since we use the after-tax cost of debt when discounting 
cash flows. Considering these savings in the cash flows as well would amount to double 
counting. 

7.3 Will increasing debt in the capital structure always a good thing since it 
increases free cash flow to equity? 
It is true that as debt increases in a firm, the free cash flow to equity also increases. However, 
this may not necessarily increase value (i.e., it is not a free lunch), because of the following 
consequences: 
• Increase in debt increases the beta and thereby the cost of equity. There will be an optimal 

debt ratio for a firm where the value is the highest. If debt is increased beyond this point, 
the increase in cash flows is more than offset by the increase in the cost of capital, thereby 
resulting in a lower value. 

• Increase in debt beyond a certain point also affects the rating of the firm and consequently 
its cost of debt.

7.5 What type of expenses should be capitalised? 
The key question whenever considering the capitalisation of expenses is to check whether 
that expense creates benefits over multiple periods.  For customer acquisition, for instance, 
the question would be that once you have spent the money acquiring a new customer, does 
that customer stay with your business for more than 1 year.  If not, then these expenses 
need to be expensed. 
An interesting case study on this is Groupon, which used to add back customer acquisition 
costs to come up with an adjusted EBITDA no. to show to investors. The only problem was 
that there was no evidence of customers staying with Groupon once they stopped that 
expense. 
Another thing to remember is that capitalising the expense does not change cash flows or 
the discount rate.  So, a legitimate question is why should we capitalise the expenses in the 
first place and what impact does it really have on value?  Capitalising changes the 
reinvestment rate which in turn changes the fundamental growth rate of earnings.  And the 
question whether that adds to value or not would depend on how efficient that expense really 
is, whether your ROC from those expenses is more than the cost of capital.

7.11 What is the best way of dealing with net operating losses? 
Some analysts value the company as if there are no losses carried forward. They then 
separately value the net operating losses simply by multiplying the NOL with the marginal tax 
rate and adding it to the value obtained earlier. However, this approach has two drawbacks: 
• It does not consider the time value of money since NOLs can be offset only against 

operating income and operating in a particular year may not be sufficient to absorb the 
entire carried forward NOL; and 

• Secondly, in case of a money losing company, it is not even certain whether the firm will 
even get the benefit of the NOL since they can be forward only for a certain number of 
years before they lapse.  

The best way then, would be not to keep operating losses separate but bring them into the 
valuation. Keep track of the NOLs that the firm is carrying forward and project income based 
on them. For instance, give the benefit of the operating losses carried forward in years when 
the income is positive. 

7.12 What is the linkage between capital expenditures and growth? 
Net capex is generally a function of how fast a firm is growing or expecting to grow. Therefore, 
assumptions about net capex can never be made independently of assumptions about growth 
in the future.

7.15 Why is cash and short-term debt excluded from computation of working capital 
for valuation purposes? 
Cash: The reason we exclude cash is because it is generally not a wasting asset. Inventories,

earns a money market rate of return (which is a fair return for a guaranteed cash flow) and 
hence is not included. If, however, cash does not earn anything, then it should be included in 
working capital. 
Short-term debt: The reason for not including short term debt is that it is included in debt. 

7.16 Why have companies started returning capital in the form of buy-backs than 
through dividends? 
Over the last few decades, especially since the 1990s, companies have increasingly returned 
capital more in the form of buy-backs than in the form of dividends.  One very plausible, but 
flawed explanation that is often given is the differential tax treatment of buy-backs and 
dividends – buy-backs being taxed at a lower rate than dividends which are taxed as ordinary 
income.  If this explanation was correct, then companies would have bought back stock more 
in the 1960s as the differential in tax rate was much higher in the 1960s than it has been 
beginning the 1990s.  Thus, the correct explanation has to do more with a change in the 1990s. 
• Employee stock options: 1990s was a year when companies started compensating 

employees a lot more through employee stock options than through cash.  Thus, 
employees’ are worth more, higher the share price of the company.  Dividends have the 
effect of lowering the price per share of the company once they are paid.  Buy-backs, on 
the other hand, have the opposite effect, i.e., they increase the price of the security.  Thus, 
as managements have started getting compensated more through stock options, they have 
increasingly resorted to buy-bacs as opposed to dividends purely out of self interest than 
anything to do with rationality.  

• Hooking up: Another possible explanation is that dividends are seen as sticky. Once a 
company starts paying a certain dividend, it feels obligated to continue that dividend policy 
even though it may not make sense for the company.  For instance, data from the US 
suggests that even in recession years, more companies increase dividends than those who 
decrease them.  Also, dividends tend to lag earnings, i.e., companies generally tend to wait 
for growth over a number of years to pass on increases in dividends to shareholders.  
Buy-backs, on the other hand, are considered irregular and hence, do not obligate a 
company to return capital through a regular buy-back program.  They provide a lot more 
flexibility to managements.   
In a nutshell, dividends are like GETTING MARRIED whereas, Buy-backs are like 
HOOKING UP!

• In the long term, the real growth rate can be higher than the real interest rate, to

Usually, assets on the balance sheet of the company should be added separately only if they

how they should be dealt with in a valuation: 
• Real estate: The value of real estate should not be included if it is being used in the 

operations of the company. 
• Vacant land: If it is not essential to the operations of the company then you should add 

the value of the vacant land to the value of the company. 
• Patents: Your revenues will already be reflecting the benefits of the patent and hence is 

already included in the value. 
• Brand name: The value of brand names should be included in the revenues and margins 

of the company. Accordingly, should not be added separately.

10.2 How should we incorporate the value of cash in a valuation? 
The simplest way to deal with cash is to keep it out of the valuation and bring it into the 
picture once the operating assets have been valued. Accordingly, the cash flows should be: 
• Before interest income from cash and marketable securities; and 
• Discount rate should not be contaminated by inclusion of cash. 
This approach will allow you to value the operating assets of the firm. Once the operating 
assets have been valued, we simply need to add the book value of cash and marketable 
securities. 
One of the main reasons for using this approach is this – If we include the interest income 
from cash in the cash flows, we would also need to adjust the discount rate for the presence 
of cash, i.e. Beta should be weighed down by the cash holdings. Unless cash remains a fixed 
percentage of value over time, these valuations will tend to break down.

11 Acquisition valuation 

11.1 Who wins in an acquisition? 
Studies have shown that acquisitions are great for the target companies but not always for the 
acquiring company stockholders.  

Proposition  
You should try to be the target than the acquirer in an acquisition because the target 

appears to consistently win (apart from the bankers to the deal of course!) 

11.2 The long-term follow-up of acquisitions 
Managers often argue that the market is unable to see the long-term benefits of mergers that 
they can see at the time of the deal. If they are right, mergers should create long term benefits 
to acquiring firms.  
However, the evidence10 does not support this hypothesis. In fact, many acquisitions are 
reversed within a fairly short period of time. 

The underlying theme in acquisitions is that growing through acquisitions seems to 
be a loser’s game. 

Forms that grow through acquisitions have generally had far more trouble creating value than 
firms that grow through internal investments. In general, acquiring firms tend to: 
• Pay too much for target firms. 
• Overestimate the value of “synergy” and “control”. 
• Have a difficult time delivering the promised results.  
Worse still, there seems to be very little learning built into the process. The same mistakes 
are made over and over again, often by the same firms with the same advisors.  

Conclusion 
There is structurally wrong with the process for acquisitions which is feeding into the 

mistakes.

11.7.1 What is synergy 

Synergy reflects the value of things that you can do as a combined company THAT YOU 
COULD NOT HAVE DONE as standalone company. Synergy requires two companies. 

11.7.2 Combining two companies makes them safer but does not necessarily add value to the 
company. How do you reconcile this? 
This is because when we value individual companies, we consider risk that we cannot diversify 
away. Accordingly, the risk which gets lowered by combining the companies is diversifiable 
risk which does not enter the valuation in the first place.  
The intuition is this:  
If a steel company buys a chemical company and tells its stockholders that it has created 
value because it is now safer, your response as a stockholder should be that I could have 
bought the chemical company myself in my portfolio and I would not have paid a 30% premium 
to do it! 
The exception to this rule is when two private companies combine with each other to create a 
safer company because we look at total risk in such cases.

12.1 Information requirements 
An analyst says that he never does a DCF valuation because it requires too many 
assumptions. He argues that it is far simpler to use a multiple obtained by looking at other 
firms in the sector, to estimate value. Is he right? 
No. Even when you are using pricing, you are implicitly making assumptions about the same 
variables as in a discounted cash flow valuation. The only difference is that in a DCF, those 
assumptions are explicit. 

12.2 Distributional assumptions 
If you estimate the PE ratio for all companies and graph out the frequency distribution, can the 
distribution be normal? 
No, because a normal distribution ranges from negative infinity to positive infinity. PE ratios 
cannot be negative. Negative PE ratios are not meaningful.

12.5 What is the “Essence of Relative Valuation” (Pricing)? 

In relative valuation, the value of an asset is compared to the values assessed by the market 
for similar or comparable assets. 
 
To do relative valuation then, 
• We need to identify comparable assets and obtain market values for these assets; 
• Convert these market values into standardized values, since the absolute prices cannot be 

compared. This process of standardizing creates price multiples; 
• Compare the standardized value or multiple for the asset being analyzed to the 

standardized values for comparable asset, controlling for any differences between firms 
that might affect the multiple, to judge whether the asset is under- or over-valued. 

12.6 What is the allure of relative valuation (why do analysts fall back on relative 
valuation rather than fundamental valuation)? 
There could be three possible reasons that people might prefer pricing over an intrinsic 
valuation: 
1. “If you think I’m crazy, you should see the guy who lives across the hall” – Jerry Seinfeld 

talking about Kramer in a Seinfeld episode  
• It’s easier to buy and sell something based on relative valuation than a discounted 

cash flow valuation.  
• Easier to create a story and compare with others than explaining yourself using pure 

numbers of a discounted cash flow valuation. 
 

2. “A little inaccuracy sometimes saves tons of explanation” – H. H. Munro 
• It’s easier to explain and defend relative valuation than a discounted cash flow 

valuation. 
 

3. “If you are going to screw up, make sure that you have lots of company” – Ex-portfolio 
manager 
• In the long term you are going to be more right with an intrinsic valuation than with the 

relative valuation. But those times you are going to be right alone. And when you are 
wrong, you are again going to be alone and you will get fired.

17 Relative valuation: Price to Earnings to Growth ratios (PEG) 

17.1 What are the challenges of using a PEG ratio? 
A PEG ratio is a PE ratio divided by growth in order to control for growth. However, there are 
two problems with the PEG ratio: 
• PEG ratio assumes a linear relation between PE and growth which is not true; and 
• PEG ratio does not control for risk and you may end up with highly risky stocks if you buy 

stocks based on a low PEG ratio basis. 

17.2 PEG ratios and fundamentals: Propositions 
Proposition 1: High risk companies will trade at much lower PEG ratios than lower risk 
companies with the same expected growth rate. 
• The company that looks most undervalued on a PEG ratio basis in a sector may be the 

riskiest firm in the sector. 
 
Proposition 2: Companies that can attain growth more efficiently by investing less in better 
return projects will have higher PEG ratios than companies that grow at the same rate less 
efficiently. 
• Companies that look cheap on a PEG ratio basis may be companies with high reinvestment 

rates and poor project returns. 
 
Proposition 3: Companies with very low or very high growth rates will tend to have higher 
PEG ratios than firms with average growth rates. This bias is worse for low growth stocks. 
• PEG ratios do not neutralize the growth effect.

22.2 What about disagreement between intrinsic and relative valuation 
Suppose a stock is trading at $15 per share and you come up with a DCF value of $10 and a 
relative value of $20. What would you do? What would your conclusion be? 
• The stock is undervalued (its price < relative value) 
• The stock is overvalued (its price > intrinsic value) 
• The stock is correctly valued (its price = average of intrinsic and relative values) 
• The stock is both undervalued and overvalued 
• None of the above 
The one thing that you should not do is try to be both a pricer as well as a valuer. 

22.3 What about disagreement between different relative valuations 
Given the above example, now suppose that you run a market regression and come up with 
a relative of $10 per share as against a value of $20 per share based on the peer group of the

• The stock is undervalued (its price < peer group relative value) 
• The stock is overvalued (its price > market relative value) 
• The stock is correctly valued (its price = average of peer and market relative values) 
• The stock is both undervalued and overvalued 
• None of the above 
The conclusion will depend on who you compare yourself to. The conclusion is simply that 
your stock is undervalued relative to one group of stocks while overvalued relative to another

23 Real Options (Introduction) 

23.1 What are real options? 
Real options are investments that are unviable today, however, they have the possibility of 
becoming viable investments in the future. For instance, think of the following situation: 
A small start-up company has come up with a new cell phone technology that promises to 
speed up download speeds and create smoother interface. The cost of implementing the 
technology is expected to be $2 billion and the present value of the expected cash flows from 
it is only $1.5 billion. Should they implement the technology now? The answer to this question 
is NO.  
However, what if you are told that you work for an established cell phone manufacturer. Would 
you be willing to pay for this currently non-viable technology, assuming that the company has 
patented it and has many years left on the patent life? The answer to this question is MAYBE. 
This is the value of the option.

24.2 What is the option to delay? 
The option to delay is the exclusive RIGHT that an organisation has to undertake a project or 
develop a product for a specified period of time. This right, then gives the firm the ability to 
delay taking the project or product until a later date. When a firm has exclusive rights to a 
project or product for a specified period of time, it can delay taking this project or product until 
a later date.  
A traditional investment analysis just answers the question of whether the project is a “good” 
one if taken TODAY.  
Thus, the fact that a project does not pass muster today, does not mean that the rights to this 
project are not valuable.

26 Real Options: Equity as an option 

26.1 Why can equity in a business be viewed as an option? 
Equity in a firm is a residual claim, i.e., equity holders lay claim to all cash flows left over after 
other financial claimholders (debt, preferred stock, etc.) have been satisfied. 
If a firm is liquidated, the same principle applies, with equity investors receiving whatever is 
left over in the firm after all outstanding debts and other financial claims are paid off. 
The principle of limited liability, however, protects equity investors in publicly traded firms if 
the value of the firm is less than the value of the outstanding debt, and they cannot lose more 
than their investment in the firm.

26.5 What is N(d1) and N(d2)? What is the probability that S>K? 
It is N(d2) which is the risk neutral probability that S>K. In some of the problems, though, we 
have used a range from N(d1) and N(d2) as the range of probabilities. And the reason is this: 
N(d1), in addition to being an option delta, is also a probability that the option will be in the 
money. In fact, the only reason d1 is different from d2 is because you are uncertain 
about S. 

𝑑2 = 𝑑1 − 	𝜎√𝑡 
If you had a standard deviation of zero, N(d1) = N(d2). As the uncertainty increases, the gap 
between these two numbers will widen. Thus, you go from being certain about the 
probability to having a range. Having said all of this, N(d2) should be the point estimate 
on the probability that S>K. You can use the range to indicate that there is uncertainty 
about this probability. 

26.6 What is the cost of delay? 
This is a tough one. Sometimes, we use 1/n and sometimes we use the cash flow next year / 
S and sometimes we use no cost of delay at all.  Let’s look at the conceptual basis. The cost 
of delay is a measure of how much you will lose in the next period if you don’t exercise the 
option now as a fraction of the current value of the underlying asset (it parallels the dividend 
yield. On a listed option on a stock, if you exercise, you will have the stock and get the 
dividends in the next period). Thus, if you have a viable oil reserve, the cost of delay is the 
cash flow you would have made on the developed reserve next period divided by the value of 
the reserve today.  
Here is the overall rule you should adopt. If you have a decent estimate of the cashflows you 
will receive each period from exercising the option, it is better to use that cashflow/ PV of the 
asset as the dividend yield.  If your cash flows are uneven or if you do not know what the 
cashflows will be each period, you should use 1/n as your cost of delay.  If you lose nothing in 
terms of cashflows by waiting, you should have no cost of delay.  

Page 86 of 115 
 



For Private Use Only 
Let me take three examples. The first is the bidding rights to televise the Olympics in an earlier 
quiz.  There were two years left to the Olympics and you were trying to price the option.  In 
this case, there is no cost of delay since you really cannot exercise the option early even if it 
is deep in the money – you cannot televise the Olympics a year before they happen.  The 
second is the oil reserve option. Since the cashflows from the reserve tend to be fairly uniform 
over time (based upon the barrels of oil you would produce and the current price per barrel, it 
is easy to estimate the cashflows you would generate each year on the reserve.  In most of 
the oil reserve problems, therefore, you would go with the cashflow/ PV of oil in the reserve 
as your cost of delay.  The third is the patent examples.  While you may be able to estimate 
the expected cashflow each year from commercialising the patent, these cashflows are more 
difficult to obtain and are less likely to be uniform over time.  That is why many of the patent 
problems use the less preferred option of 1/n as the cost of delay, where n is the number of 
years left in the patent.  
Early exercise and the cost of delay: With listed options, you are almost always better off 
holding or selling an option than exercising it. That is why most options can be valued through 
their stated maturities. With real options, though, not exercising until expiration sometimes 
makes no sense, since the value of the real option comes from exercise. Thus, if you have the 
right to develop an oil reserve for the next 20 years, it does not make sense to wait until the 
last day of the twentieth year to develop the reserve, since you will never get the oil out in a 
day. That is why you see a cost of delay introduced as a counterweight. Intuitively, think of 
this as the cost of not exercising a real option, once it becomes viable. Thus, the cost of delay 
with a viable oil reserve is the cash flow that you would get by developing it immediately and 
if that cash flow exceeds the time premium on the option, you will develop. This cost of delay 
operates like a dividend on a stock, with options outstanding, and that is why you have to use 
the dividend-yield adjusted option pricing model. 
Ultimately, the real insight you get from real options is not in the option pricing models but in 
the intuition. They explain why vulture investors buy equity in the most risky distressed sectors 
and not the least risky, why companies may pay large amounts for non-viable technologies 
and why pharmaceuticals may hold off on developing viable drugs. They may also explain why 
you pay premiums for some young technology companies, over and above the discounted 
cash flow value. Until next time!

33.3 The investment banking guarantee 

Almost all IPOs are managed by investment banks and are backed by a pricing guarantee, 
where the investment banker guarantees the offering price to the issuer.  
If the price at which the issuance is made is lower than the guaranteed price, the investment 
banker will buy shares at the guaranteed price and potentially bear the loss.  

34 Private to VC to Public 

34.1 Propositions 
Proposition 1: The value of a private business that is expected to transition to a publicly 
traded company will be higher than the value of an otherwise similar private business that 
does not expect to make this transition. 
• Private businesses in sectors that are “hot” in terms of going public (think social media in 

2014) will be worth more than private businesses in less sexy areas; 
• As IPOs boom (bust) private company valuations will increase (decrease); 
• Private companies in countries that have easy access to public markets will have higher 

value than companies in countries without that access.  
 
Proposition 2: The value of a private business that expects to make the transition to a public 
company sooner will be higher than the value of an otherwise similar company that will take 
longer.  
• Private businesses will be worth more if companies are able to go public earlier in their life 

cycle.

Appendix A: Primer on option pricing models 
The driving force in all of option pricing is the notion of a REPLICATING PORTFOLIO. 

Prior to 1971 
• Value the option like any other asset: expected cash flows, project out the cash flows, 

adjust for probabilities and take the present value of the expected cash flows to come up 
with a value of the option. 

• Therefore, options were valued like a normal stock or a bond.  

Post 1971 (Fischer Black and Myron Scholes) 
• The insight: We can create something that looks exactly like an option in terms of cash 

flows by using the underlying asset and either borrowing or lending what is called the 
replicating portfolio.  

• The principle of arbitrage: If you have two investments with the same cash flows, they 
should trade at the SAME PRICE. In every option pricing model, you try to: 
o create a replicating portfolio; 
o see how much it will cost you to create that replicating portfolio; and 
o The option has to trade at that same value because if it did not, you have a riskless way 

of making money. 
Therefore, every option pricing model is a search for that replicating portfolio.  

Creating a replicating portfolio 
The objective in creating a replicating portfolio is to use a combination of risk-free borrowing/ 
lending and the underlying asset to create the same cash flows as the option being valued: 
• Call option = Borrowing + Buying D of underlying stock 
• Put option = Lending + Selling short D on underlying asset

The “sampling” problem 
However, both the above studies suffer rom a significant sampling bias: 
• The companies that make restricted stock offerings are likely to be small, troubled firms 

that have run out of conventional financing options. 
• The types of IPOs where equity investors sell their stake in the five months prior to the IPO 

at a huge discount are likely to be IPOs that have significant pricing uncertainty associated 
with them. 

In fact, the magnitude of the sampling bias was estimated by comparing the discount on all 
private placements to the discount on restricted stock offerings. One study concluded that the 
“illiquidity” alone accounted for less than 10%, leaving the remaining 20% - 25% to be 
explained by sampling problems.
